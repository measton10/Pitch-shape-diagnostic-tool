Methodology

Four models, one command layer, and one composite grade — explained without the jargon.

Each model studies thousands of real MLB pitches and learns what a pitch's shape (spin, break, velocity) should produce. It then predicts an outcome for every pitch and compares that to what actually happened — the difference is called a "gap," the number used throughout this project. The AUC score above shows how well each model actually learned: 0.5 means random guessing, 1.0 means perfect prediction.

Whiff Model

Does it miss bats? The most direct measure of "stuff." A pitch can excel here and still be hittable if contact happens.
How we know: Statcast tags every pitch with its exact outcome — we check for the specific "swinging strike" tag.

Hard-Hit Model

When contact happens, how hard? A pitch can get fewer swings-and-misses but still avoid barrels.
How we know: Statcast's tracking system measures the ball's exact speed off the bat — 95+ mph counts as hard-hit, the real MLB threshold.

Ground Ball Model

Where does contact go? A pitch built for weak grounders can look "bad" by whiff rate alone and still be doing its job.
How we know: the same tracking system measures the angle the ball leaves the bat — under 10° counts as a grounder.

Barrel Model

Is the contact truly damaging? The strictest standard — MLB's real definition of dangerous contact.
How we know: Statcast classifies every batted ball on an official 1–6 quality scale combining exit velocity and angle — a 6 is an official barrel.
Where the data comes from: the four models above are trained on league-wide data — thousands of pitches from hundreds of different MLB pitchers, not any one individual. Once trained, that league-wide model is applied to specific real pitchers to see how their actual results compare to what their shape predicts. This project validates that process against three real 2024 pitchers: Paul Skenes, Framber Valdez, and Kyle Hendricks — see the Validation page for full results.

The ground ball model performs best because vertical break has the most direct physical link to whether a ball comes off the bat high or low — a cleaner relationship than swing-and-miss or contact quality, which depend more on timing and deception. All four are Random Forest models trained on real 2024 MLB Statcast data.

The Command Layer

Shape alone doesn't tell the whole story — where a pitch is thrown matters just as much. Two zones are tracked for every pitch: the chase zone (just off the plate, hard to do damage on) and the heart zone (the most hittable part of the strike zone, dead center). A pitch with great shape but a high heart-zone rate points to a command problem, not a stuff problem — and vice versa.

The Composite Grade

All four models combine into one number per pitch, scaled the way real scouting grades work: 100 = league average. A grade of 120 means a pitch performs meaningfully better than its shape alone would predict; 80 means meaningfully worse. This is the single number a coach could scan in five seconds, backed by the full three-model breakdown underneath it. Each pitcher's individual gap is converted to a z-score relative to the league-wide distribution for that pitch type, then all three z-scores are averaged into the final grade.
